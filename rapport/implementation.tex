\section{Implementation}

This section describes the concrete realization of the ultrasound-based RTLS prototype, covering the hardware topology, the choice of sensor modules, the firmware running on each processing unit, and the inter-device communication protocols.

\subsection{Hardware Architecture}

The system is built around two microcontroller units: a \textbf{Teensy~4.0}, responsible for distance acquisition and position computation, and an \textbf{ESP32}, responsible for receiving and persistently logging the computed positions. Four ultrasonic sensor modules are used as anchors, each communicating with the Teensy over a dedicated UART channel. The computed position estimate is then forwarded to the ESP32 over a fifth UART link. Figure~\ref{fig:hw-arch} illustrates the overall hardware topology.

\subsubsection{Ultrasonic Sensor Modules}

All four sensor modules used in this system are waterproof ultrasonic ranging sensors communicating exclusively over UART, making them well-suited for embedded systems with limited GPIO availability. The modules are physically installed at the corners of the monitored area to act as anchor points, in a $4\,\text{m} \times 4\,\text{m}$ square layout.

\vskip .3cm
\noindent\textbf{AJ-SR04M}

\noindent
Three of the four anchors use the \textbf{AJ-SR04M} waterproof ultrasonic sensor module. It operates at a nominal frequency of $40\,\text{kHz}$, a supply voltage in the range $3.0$--$5.5\,\text{V}_\text{DC}$, and a quiescent current below $8\,\text{mA}$. Its key ranging specifications are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:aj-sr04m-specs}.

        \midrule
        Operating frequency        & $40\,\text{kHz}$ \\
        Measuring range            & $20$--$600\,\text{cm}$ \\

        Accuracy                   & $\pm\,1\,\text{cm}$ \\

        \bottomrule

The AJ-SR04M supports several operating modes, selectable via an on-board resistor (R17). We use \textbf{Mode~4} (UART query mode), in which a measurement is taken only when explicitly requested by the host. This choice is motivated by two considerations. First, a query-based scheme gives the firmware precise control over measurement timing and sequencing, which is essential for interrogating four anchors in turn without accumulating stale readings. Second, the UART interface requires only two signal lines (RX/TX) rather than dedicated GPIO lines for the trigger pulse and echo capture needed in the standard HC-SR04-compatible modes, simplifying the wiring to the Teensy.

In Mode~4, a measurement is initiated by sending the trigger byte \texttt{0x01} over the module's RX line. The module responds with a four-byte frame: a fixed start byte \texttt{0xFF}, a high data byte, a low data byte, and a checksum byte computed as the sum of the high and low bytes. The measured distance in millimetres is recovered as:
\[
    d = (\text{H\_byte} \ll 8) \mid \text{L\_byte}
\]

\vskip .3cm
\noindent\textbf{SR04M-2}

\noindent
The fourth anchor uses the \textbf{SR04M-2} module, which is functionally equivalent to the JSN-SR04T~2.0. Like the AJ-SR04M, it consists of a waterproof transducer probe (rated IP67) connected to a control board, and operates at $40\,\text{kHz}$. Its key ranging specifications are listed in Table~\ref{tab:sr04m2-specs}.

The SR04M-2 also supports multiple modes, selected via the R19 resistor footprint. We use \textbf{Mode~3} (UART query mode), for the same reasons that motivate the choice of Mode~4 on the AJ-SR04M: on-demand triggering and a two-wire UART interface. The UART protocol of the SR04M-2 differs slightly from the AJ-SR04M in two respects. The measurement is triggered by sending the byte \texttt{0x55} instead of \texttt{0x01}, and the checksum is computed as the sum of \emph{all three} preceding bytes --- including the start byte \texttt{0xFF} --- rather than only the high and low bytes. In all other respects (frame structure, distance encoding) the two protocols are identical.

\subsubsection{Sensor Characterisation and Deployment Scenarios}
\label{subsec:sensor-char}

Despite the nominal range of up to $600\,\text{cm}$ advertised by both sensor models, practical measurements reveal a significant asymmetry between the two: the AJ-SR04M anchors reliably detect distances up to $4000\,\text{mm}$, while the SR04M-2 saturates beyond $2500\,\text{mm}$. Furthermore, both models exhibit a consistent distance-dependent underestimation bias, well described by a linear model. Table~\ref{tab:distance_calibration} reports the raw UART output of all four anchors at known ground-truth distances.

Fitting a linear regression of the form $\text{measured} = \alpha \cdot \text{true} + \beta$ to each anchor's data yields:
\begin{align*}
    \text{meas}_1 &= 0.9896\times\text{true} - 21.5 \\
    \text{meas}_2 &= 0.9876\times\text{true} - 12.6 \\
    \text{meas}_3 &= 0.9713\times\text{true} - 19.9 \\
    \text{meas}_4 &= 1.0700\times\text{true} - 43.8
\end{align*}
These corrections are inverted and applied in real time by the sensor driver, as the \texttt{correct\_error} callback stored in each \texttt{sensor\_t} instance.

The asymmetry in effective range between the AJ-SR04M ($4000\,\text{mm}$) and the SR04M-2 ($2500\,\text{mm}$) gives rise to two distinct deployment scenarios.

\vskip .3cm
\noindent\textbf{Four-anchor deployment (room up to $1.76\,\text{m} \times 1.76\,\text{m}$)}

\noindent
When all four anchors are active, the operational envelope is bounded by the SR04M-2's effective range of $2500\,\text{mm}$. In a square room of side $L$ with anchors at the four corners, the greatest possible anchor-to-target distance is the half-diagonal $L\sqrt{2}$. Requiring this not to exceed $2500\,\text{mm}$ gives:
\[
    L\sqrt{2} \leq 2500 \implies L \leq \frac{2500}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 1768\,\text{mm}
\]
The supported room area is therefore at most approximately $1.76\,\text{m}\times 1.76\,\text{m}$.

In this configuration, the recommended positioning algorithm is \textbf{MLE with 4 iterations} (MLE-4). As evidenced by the results in Section~4, MLE-4 achieves the best accuracy among all four-anchor methods while remaining computationally negligible ($6.7\,\mu\text{s}$ worst-case on the Teensy~4.0). OLS is discarded because, although it also uses four anchors, MLE provides significantly superior accuracy; FGLS requires a priori knowledge of the measurement noise variance $\sigma^2$, which cannot be determined in practice without a controlled calibration procedure, rendering it infeasible in a deployment context.

\vskip .3cm
\noindent\textbf{Three-anchor deployment (room up to $2.8\,\text{m}\times 2.8\,\text{m}$)}

\noindent
If the SR04M-2 anchor is omitted, the system operates with the three AJ-SR04M anchors only, extending the range to $4000\,\text{mm}$. Applying the same diagonal constraint:
\[
    L\sqrt{2} \leq 4000 \implies L \leq \frac{4000}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 2828\,\text{mm}
\]
A room of up to approximately $2.8\,\text{m}\times 2.8\,\text{m}$ is therefore supported. In this configuration, the viable algorithms are \textbf{trilateration} and \textbf{MLE} (adapted to three anchors). Trilateration yields a closed-form, near-instantaneous estimate ($57\,\text{ns}$), while MLE-8 achieves a lower mean error at the cost of a modest increase in computation time ($11\,\mu\text{s}$). OLS and FGLS are not applicable in this scenario for the same reasons as above: OLS is defined only for four or more anchors, and FGLS requires a known $\sigma^2$.

\subsubsection{Teensy~4.0}

The Teensy~4.0 serves as the central processing unit of the system, fulfilling three distinct roles: sensor interrogation, position computation, and result transmission. The assignment of this role to the Teensy --- rather than to the ESP32 --- is directly motivated by the hardware capabilities of each board.

It is based on the NXP i.MX RT1062 system-on-chip, running an ARM Cortex-M7 core at $600\,\text{MHz}$ with a single-precision hardware floating-point unit (FPU) and a hardware cycle counter --- accessed through the \texttt{ARM\_DWT\_CYCCNT} register --- which is used in the firmware to profile algorithm execution times. The Teensy~4.0 exposes \textbf{7 hardware UART peripherals} (\texttt{Serial1}--\texttt{Serial7}), in addition to a dedicated USB serial interface (\texttt{Serial}) used as a serial monitor during development. Our application requires six serial interfaces in total: four hardware UARTs for the sensor anchors (\texttt{Serial1}--\texttt{Serial4}), one hardware UART for the communication link to the ESP32 (\texttt{Serial5}), and the USB serial interface (\texttt{Serial}) for monitoring and debugging. The Teensy's 7 available hardware UARTs comfortably accommodate this requirement.

\subsubsection{ESP32}

The ESP32 acts as a data logger. It receives timestamped position packets from the Teensy over a UART link and persistently stores them to its internal flash memory, organised as a LittleFS filesystem, in a CSV-formatted log file. Entries are accumulated in an in-memory double buffer and flushed to flash in batches, so as to reduce the number of write operations and protect the flash from excessive wear.

The assignment of the logger role to the ESP32 is justified by the same hardware argument. The ESP32 exposes only \textbf{3 hardware UART peripherals}, which would be wholly insufficient for the sensor interrogation role --- that role demands at least 4 UARTs for the anchors alone, plus one additional link to a logging device. The logging role, by contrast, requires only a single UART interface (to receive data from the Teensy), which fits within the ESP32's capabilities. Furthermore, the ESP32 is well-suited to future wireless extensions of the system, as it natively integrates Wi-Fi and Bluetooth transceivers, enabling potential cloud or LAN logging through the stub \texttt{log\_cloud} module already present in the firmware.

\subsection{Firmware}

The firmware for both microcontrollers is written in C++17 and built with the \textbf{PlatformIO} build system, using the Arduino framework as the hardware abstraction layer. The project defines two PlatformIO build environments in \texttt{platformio.ini}: \texttt{tracker} targeting the \texttt{teensy40} board, and \texttt{logger} targeting the \texttt{esp32dev} board. Source files are partitioned accordingly between the \texttt{src/teensy/} and \texttt{src/esp32/} directories, and the corresponding subdirectories under \texttt{include/} expose their respective public interfaces.

\subsubsection{Teensy Firmware}

The Teensy firmware is organised into three modules: the sensor driver, the position estimation library, and the logging interface.

\vskip .3cm
\noindent\textbf{Sensor driver (\texttt{sensor.cpp/h})}

\noindent
The sensor driver provides a uniform interface for interrogating any of the four anchors, abstracting over the differences in trigger bytes and checksum schemes between the two sensor models. Each anchor is described by a \texttt{sensor\_t} structure, which bundles together a serial handle, a trigger byte, a pointer to a calibration function, and a pointer to a checksum function. The serial handle is a tagged union wrapping either a hardware (\texttt{HardwareSerial}) or software (\texttt{SoftwareSerial}) serial object, enabling both hardware and software serial ports to be used transparently.

Two functions are exposed:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \texttt{sensor\_begin(index, baud)}: initialises the serial interface of the sensor at the given index at the specified baud rate.
    \item \texttt{read\_sensor(index)}: triggers a measurement, waits for the four-byte response with a $100\,\text{ms}$ timeout, validates the checksum, applies the sensor-specific calibration correction, and returns the corrected distance in millimetres as a 32-bit signed integer, or $-1$ on failure.
\end{itemize}

The calibration corrections are linear functions fitted from empirical measurements taken at known distances (see Section~\ref{subsec:sensor-char} and Table~\ref{tab:distance_calibration}), and are defined in \texttt{include/teensy/config.h}: for instance, anchor~1's correction is $\text{corrected} = (\text{raw} + 21.5) / 0.9896$.

\vskip .3cm
\noindent\textbf{Position estimation (\texttt{position.cpp/h})}

\noindent
The position estimation module implements four positioning algorithms, each taking as input a four-element array of measured distances (in mm) and writing its two-dimensional position estimate (in mm) to a caller-supplied output buffer. All four algorithms are founded on the linearisation technique detailed in Section~3: the squared norm identity $\Vert x - a_k \Vert^2 = d_k^2$ is differenced against the first anchor's equation to eliminate the quadratic term in $x$, yielding a linear system in the target coordinates. The constant anchor positions and the precomputable matrix quantities derived therefrom are declared \texttt{static constexpr} and therefore evaluated entirely at compile time, incurring no runtime overhead.

The four algorithms exposed are:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \texttt{position\_trilateration}: exact inversion of the $2\times2$ edge matrix $E$, using only three anchors.
    \item \texttt{position\_ols}: ordinary least squares, $\hat{t}_\text{OLS} = (E^TE)^{-1}E^Ty$, using all four anchors.
    \item \texttt{position\_fgls}: feasible generalized least squares, $\hat{t}_\text{FGLS} = (E^T\hat\Psi^{-1}E)^{-1}E^T\hat\Psi^{-1}y$, using an estimated covariance matrix $\hat\Psi$ constructed from the measured distances and the noise variance $\sigma^2$; the OLS estimate is used as a warm start.
    \item \texttt{position\_mle}: numerical maximum likelihood estimation via Gauss--Newton iterations on the nonlinear residual $\tilde d(x) - \text{dist}$, where $\tilde d_k(x) = \Vert x - a_k\Vert$; the Jacobian is approximated by finite differences with step $h = 10^{-2}\,\text{mm}$.
\end{itemize}

Matrix operations (matrix--vector product, matrix--matrix product, $2\times2$ and $3\times3$ inversion via cofactor expansion) are implemented as standalone helper functions operating on flat, row-major arrays of \texttt{float}, with explicit size parameters, so that the compiler can fully unroll all loops at the small fixed dimensions used.

\vskip .3cm
\noindent\textbf{Logging interface (\texttt{log.cpp/h})}

\noindent
The logging module is responsible for transmitting computed position estimates to the ESP32 over the UART link on \texttt{Serial5}. A single function is exposed: \texttt{log\_timepos(timestamp, pos)}, which serialises a \texttt{log\_packet\_t} structure, sends it, and waits for the ESP32's acknowledgement before returning. The packet format is described in Section~\ref{subsec:uart-protocol}.

\subsubsection{ESP32 Firmware}

The ESP32 firmware follows a producer-consumer architecture built on FreeRTOS, the real-time operating system embedded in the ESP-IDF and exposed through the Arduino framework for ESP32. Two tasks run concurrently:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{\texttt{uart\_recieve}}: continuously polls the UART receive buffer for incoming \texttt{log\_packet\_t} frames. Upon reception, it validates the start byte and checksum; malformed packets are discarded and counted. Valid packets are queued and, once the active queue slot is full, the logger task is notified via \texttt{xTaskNotify}.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{logger\_task}}: blocks indefinitely on \texttt{xTaskNotifyWait}. Upon notification, it flushes the completed slot of the double buffer to the LittleFS log file \texttt{/log.csv} by appending one CSV row per entry, of the form \texttt{timestamp,x,y}.
\end{itemize}

The double-buffer mechanism uses two fixed-size arrays of \texttt{log\_queue\_entry\_t} structures (of depth \texttt{QUEUE\_SIZE}~$= 10$), indexed by an atomic integer. When the active buffer fills up, the atomic index is flipped via an XOR operation, atomically switching the roles of the two buffers, and the logger task is notified with the index of the full buffer. This allows the reception task to continue writing to the newly active buffer without contention while the logger task flushes to flash, eliminating any lock overhead on the critical path. The log file is initialised with a CSV header row (\texttt{timestamp,x,y}) upon first creation, and subsequently opened in append mode for each flush.

\subsection{Inter-Device Communication Protocols}
\label{subsec:uart-protocol}

\subsubsection{Sensor UART Protocol}

All four sensor modules are interrogated over hardware UART at $9600\,\text{bps}$. Prior to issuing a trigger, any stale data in the receive buffer is discarded. The trigger byte is then written to the sensor's RX line; the module responds with a four-byte frame within at most $100\,\text{ms}$:

\[
\underbrace{\texttt{0xFF}}_{\text{start}} \quad
\underbrace{H}_{\text{high byte}} \quad
\underbrace{L}_{\text{low byte}} \quad
\underbrace{\mathit{cs}}_{\text{checksum}}
\]

The checksum scheme differs by model: $\mathit{cs} = H + L$ for the AJ-SR04M, and $\mathit{cs} = \texttt{0xFF} + H + L$ for the SR04M-2. The distance in mm is decoded as $(H \ll 8) \mid L$.

\subsubsection{Teensy-to-ESP32 UART Protocol}

Position data is transmitted from the Teensy (\texttt{Serial5}) to the ESP32 at $115200\,\text{bps}$ using a custom binary framing protocol with acknowledgement. The link operates in a request--reply fashion: for each computed position, the Teensy sends one \texttt{log\_packet\_t} and waits up to $100\,\text{ms}$ for an \texttt{ack\_packet\_t} before proceeding.

The data frame (\texttt{log\_packet\_t}) is a packed, 14-byte structure laid out as follows:

\begin{table}[ht]
    \centering
    \caption{Layout of the \texttt{log\_packet\_t} data frame sent from the Teensy to the ESP32.}
    \label{tab:log-packet}
    \begin{tabular}{cccc}
        \toprule
        Field & Type & Size (bytes) & Description \\
        \midrule
        \texttt{start}     & \texttt{uint8\_t}  & 1 & Start byte, always \texttt{0xAA} \\
        \texttt{timestamp} & \texttt{uint32\_t} & 4 & Millisecond timestamp (\texttt{millis()}) \\
        \texttt{pos[0]}    & \texttt{float}     & 4 & Estimated $x$-coordinate (mm) \\
        \texttt{pos[1]}    & \texttt{float}     & 4 & Estimated $y$-coordinate (mm) \\
        \texttt{checksum}  & \texttt{uint8\_t}  & 1 & 8-bit sum of all preceding bytes \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Upon receiving a valid frame, the ESP32 replies with a 5-byte acknowledgement frame (\texttt{ack\_packet\_t}) composed of the start byte \texttt{0xAA} followed by the echoed 4-byte timestamp. The Teensy verifies both the start byte and the timestamp before considering the transmission successful. If no valid acknowledgement arrives within the timeout, the transmission is abandoned and the system proceeds to the next measurement cycle.
